**Short answer.** Choose WordPress when the site is mainly published content (pages, a blog, news) and a standard theme with a few well-maintained plugins covers what you need. Choose a custom build when the site carries your own business logic: workflows, integrations, accounts, bilingual structure or performance targets that plugins can only approximate.
</Callout>

A disclosure first: we build custom websites and applications, not WordPress sites. Weigh what follows with that in mind. The aim of this article is not to talk you out of WordPress. It is to help you see which kind of project you have, because the answer is different for each.

## What WordPress is good at

WordPress is the most widely used content management system on the web. [W3Techs](https://w3techs.com/technologies/details/cm-wordpress) reports it on roughly two in five of all websites, and on more than half of the sites whose CMS it can identify. That reach has real benefits:

- **A large talent pool.** Almost any web agency or freelancer can work on it.
- **A familiar editor.** Many marketing teams have used it before.
- **Ready-made plugins.** Contact forms, SEO fields, galleries and simple shops are available off the shelf.
- **A fast start.** A content site that fits an existing theme can launch quickly.

If your site is a set of pages and articles that your team edits often, and nothing about it is unusual, WordPress is a reasonable choice. We say the same in our [FAQ](/faq): templates trade flexibility for speed, and for a blog that trade is often worth it.

## Where the trade-off turns

The trade-off turns when the site has to do things that are specific to your business. The usual signs:

1. Your workflow is your own: quotes, bookings, approvals or client portals that follow your process rather than a plugin's.
2. You depend on local integrations: regional payment providers, logistics services or SMS gateways that have no maintained plugin, or one that only approximates their API.
3. Two languages carry equal weight: Arabic and English with different layouts, different content and correct right-to-left behaviour, not a translation plugin layered on top. See [how bilingual architecture works](/writing/multilingual-architecture).
4. Performance is a requirement, not a hope: you need to hold [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) thresholds on every page, every release.
5. You expect to grow the system: dashboards, accounts or an API later, without starting over.

When two or more of these apply, each plugin you add to close a gap becomes a dependency you do not control. That is the point where a custom build can cost less over the life of the system than it appears to at the start.

## Security: where the risk actually sits

WordPress core is maintained carefully. The risk sits mostly in the ecosystem around it. Patchstack's [State of WordPress Security in 2025](https://patchstack.com/whitepaper/state-of-wordpress-security-in-2025/) reports two figures for 2024:

- 7,966 new vulnerabilities in the WordPress ecosystem, **96% of them in plugins**, 4% in themes and 7 individual issues in core.
- A third of the vulnerabilities it tracked had no fix available when they were publicly disclosed.

What that means in practice:

- Every plugin is code written by someone else, updated on their schedule.
- A well-run WordPress site needs someone to review and apply updates continuously.
- A site with few, well-maintained plugins carries far less risk than one with many.

A custom build does not remove security work. It changes its shape: fewer third-party moving parts, and a smaller surface you can review in full.

## Performance: what drives it

A WordPress site can be fast. Whether it is depends on the theme, the number of plugins loading scripts on every page, and the hosting. A custom build gives you control over each of those choices, and lets you check speed against [published thresholds](/standards) before a release goes out. Either way, measure the result with [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) on real pages, not on a demo.

## Ownership and lock-in

With WordPress, you own your content and can move hosts. You do not own the plugins your site depends on; if a vendor changes its licence or stops updating, you inherit the problem.

With a custom build, what you should own is everything: source code, database, configuration and accounts.

That is how we hand over. At final payment the repository moves to an account you control, with the database schema and migration history, an environment and secrets guide, and deployment documentation. The [FAQ](/faq) lists it in full. Whoever you hire, ask for the same.

## Moving from WordPress to a custom build

If you decide to move, the risk is in the migration, not the new design. A safe sequence:

1. **Inventory what exists.** Pages, posts, media, forms, integrations and which URLs receive traffic.
2. **Map every URL.** Decide where each old URL lives on the new site.
3. **Redirect permanently.** Use server-side 301 redirects from each old URL to its new home, as [Google's site move guide](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/site-move-with-url-changes) recommends.
4. **Migrate content with structure.** Move content into a model that matches how your team edits, not a copy of the old one.
5. **Check after launch.** Watch Search Console for crawl errors and fix any missed redirects.

If you are unsure whether the current site is worth keeping, start with a [technical audit](/writing/technical-audit-before-rebuild) rather than a rebuild.

## Self-check: which side is your project on?

Answer yes or no:

- Does the site mainly publish pages and articles?
- Does a standard theme cover the design you need?
- Are your integrations common ones with maintained plugins?
- Is one language enough, or is the second one a light translation?
- Do you have someone to apply updates every week?

Mostly yes: WordPress is likely the better fit. Mostly no: a custom build deserves a serious look.

## WordPress vs custom website: common questions

### Is WordPress bad for SEO?

No. Search engines do not rank a site lower for using WordPress. What matters is content, structure, speed and correct technical setup, and all of those can be good or bad on either approach.

### Is a custom website more secure than WordPress?

Not automatically. A custom site is only as secure as its code and its maintenance. Its advantage is a smaller, known surface without third-party plugins, which makes it easier to review and keep current.

### Can I still edit content myself on a custom website?

Yes, if it is built with a content management layer. Ask whoever builds it which parts your team can edit without a developer, and see it working before you sign.

### Is a custom website always more expensive?

At the start, usually yes. Over several years the comparison depends on how much plugin licensing, workaround development and rework the WordPress route would need. See [pricing](/pricing) for the published ranges of a custom build.
